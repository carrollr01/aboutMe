# Writer's Brief — Relationship Resurrection

Anyone writing a chapter for this book must read this first. Voice consistency matters more than any single sentence.

## What this book is

A practical, confrontational manual for long-term couples whose relationship has gone numb. The thesis: domestic comfort is the slow killer. The cure isn't communication workbooks or attachment-style worksheets — it's deliberate disruption, embodied attention, and re-introducing risk, novelty, and admiration back into a relationship that has settled into roommate physics.

The book is split into three parts:
1. **His Resurrection** — chapters for men. What he stopped doing, what he became, how he gets back.
2. **Her Resurrection** — chapters for women. What she stopped wanting, what she started managing, how she returns.
3. **The Resurrection Together** — the joint protocol, the vow, the path forward.

Plus four appendices.

## Voice

Read the source X post. Match its **energy and structure**. Do not match its tics.

**KEEP:**
- Urgency. Every chapter reads like the reader's relationship is on fire and you are the only person willing to say it.
- Vivid, embodied imagery. Not abstractions. "She used to tremble when you walked in" beats "she felt attraction."
- Short paragraphs. One-line punches. Rhythm. Read it aloud — if it doesn't have a pulse, rewrite it.
- Confrontational second-person. "You did this. You buried it. You can dig it out."
- A villain in the background: the modern world, the comfort trap, the wellness-industrial complex that profits when love dies. Reference it sparingly — once or twice per chapter.
- The "two lovers are a dangerous force" thesis. Restate it in new ways. Don't repeat the exact phrasing.

**CUT:**
- Gratuitous profanity. The source post drops "fuck" as filler. We don't. Use a curse word only when it's the right word and lands the line. As a rule: at most a few across an entire chapter, and never as adjective filler ("fucking laundry," "fuck this world"). When in doubt, leave it out — restraint reads stronger than constant heat.
- Edgy cheap shots ("cardigan-wearing fat bitch named Dr. Sarah"). The contempt for therapy-as-content is fine; the personal slur is cheap and weakens the argument. Punch the institution, not a fictional person.
- "Fuckworse," "fuckin," and other stylistic tics that read as performance.
- "Bro," "king," "queen," "alpha," manosphere vocabulary. This is sharper than that.
- Empty intensifiers ("insane," "crazy," "literally"). Be specific instead.

**ADD:**
- Real science, woven in as prose, not bolted on. Mention researchers by name in the sentence ("Esther Perel's work on eroticism in long-term relationships," "John Gottman's lab found that..."). No footnotes, no citation apparatus, no parenthetical years. It should read like someone who has read widely and is now talking to you at a bar — not someone who is performing scholarship.
- Be willing to challenge the science where it deserves it. Ego depletion didn't replicate well. The 55/38/7 nonverbal stat is misused. The point isn't to genuflect at studies — it's to bring receipts that back the thesis.

## Evidence layer — who to draw on

Don't cite every one of these in every chapter. Pick the two or three that genuinely sharpen the argument.

- **Esther Perel** — "Mating in Captivity." Domesticity and eroticism pull in opposite directions. Distance is the precondition for desire.
- **John Gottman** — The Four Horsemen (contempt, criticism, defensiveness, stonewalling). The 5:1 positive-to-negative interaction ratio in stable couples. Bids for connection and turning toward. The Love Lab predictions.
- **Helen Fisher** — Three brain systems: lust (testosterone), romantic attraction (dopamine, norepinephrine), and attachment (oxytocin, vasopressin). Long-term couples who still report being "in love" show dopaminergic activity similar to early-stage couples.
- **Arthur & Elaine Aron** — Self-expansion theory. The 36 questions. Novel + arousing shared activities increase reported relationship quality. The shaky bridge / misattribution of arousal studies (Dutton & Aron).
- **David Schnarch** — Differentiation. The "crucible." Desire requires two separate selves, not two halves of one fused identity.
- **Sue Johnson** — Emotionally Focused Therapy. Attachment in adult romantic bonds. "Hold Me Tight."
- **Roy Baumeister** — Sexual desire frequency mismatch research. Men report higher baseline desire; the gap is partly biological, partly socially shaped. (Be honest about the controversies.)
- **Eli Finkel** — "The All-or-Nothing Marriage." Modern marriages demand more from a single partner than any previous era and often deliver less.
- **Amy Muise** — Sexual communal motivation. Couples who have sex to meet a partner's needs (without resentment) report higher satisfaction. The "afterglow" research showing post-sex bonding lasts about 48 hours.
- **Shelly Gable** — Active-constructive responding to good news. How couples handle each other's wins predicts longevity better than how they handle conflict.
- **James Coan** — fMRI hand-holding study. Threat response drops measurably when a spouse holds the subject's hand. Stranger's hand: less effect. Hostile marriage: no effect.
- **Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi** — Flow. Shared flow experiences are bonding.
- **The Coolidge effect** — Animal studies on novelty and sexual response. Translate carefully to humans.
- **Daniel Kahneman** — Peak-end rule. Couples remember the peaks and the endings of experiences, not the averages. Date design implication: engineer peaks.
- **Bowlby / Ainsworth / Hazan & Shaver** — Adult attachment styles. Anxious, avoidant, secure. Use sparingly — the manosphere has mangled this. Stick to what the research actually says.
- **Christensen & Heavey** — The demand-withdraw pattern. Almost always a woman demanding, a man withdrawing.
- **Drigotas & Rusbult** — The Michelangelo phenomenon. Partners shape each other toward their ideal selves. Affirmation as sculpture.

You can also use:
- **Sapolsky** on chronic stress and cortisol's effect on libido.
- **Robin Dunbar** on grooming, touch, and bonding.
- **Terri Conley** on female desire research that pushed back on the old assumptions.
- **Daniel Gilbert** on hedonic adaptation — why the new car (and the new relationship) stops feeling new.

If you reach for something and aren't certain it exists or who said it, **leave it out.** Fabricated studies will destroy trust in this book faster than anything else.

## Chapter structure

Each chapter:
1. **Opening hook** — a scene, a question, a punch. 200–400 words. No throat-clearing.
2. **The diagnosis** — what's actually happening, why, what the science says. This is where the named researchers come in.
3. **The argument** — what to do about it, why this works, what the resistance will be.
4. **APPLICATION** — a concrete section labeled "APPLICATION." This is the deliverable. Specific actions. Today actions. This-week actions. Not journaling. Not "reflect on how this made you feel." Things the reader does that the partner notices.

Target length per chapter: **3,000–5,000 words.** Cut anything that's not earning its place.

## What men's chapters and women's chapters should and shouldn't do

Both halves are written *to* the reader of that half, but they are also being read by the other partner. This is intentional. The man's chapters can be sharp with him; the woman's can be sharp with her. Neither half should be a brief against the other partner. The frame is "this is what you stopped doing." Not "this is what she did to you."

When you write the men's half, don't make the women a villain. When you write the women's half, don't make the men a villain. Both halves accuse the reader — that's the whole point.

## What to avoid completely

- Therapy-speak: "hold space," "attachment style language," "trauma response" used loosely.
- Self-help bromides: "love yourself first," "communication is everything."
- Religious framing beyond the occasional dramatic Biblical reference the source uses (Adam and Eve, the Garden). Don't make it a Christian relationship book.
- Gender essentialism dressed up as science. Mention testosterone/estrogen where genuinely relevant; don't extrapolate to destiny.
- Anything that looks AI-generated: tricolons in every paragraph, "it's not just X — it's Y," "let's dive in," "in today's fast-paced world." Cut on sight.

## Style touchstones

If you've read these, channel them in this rough mix:
- The directness of David Goggins, without the screaming.
- The specificity and rhythm of Mark Manson at his best, with less snark.
- Esther Perel's intelligence about what desire actually requires.
- Cormac McCarthy's economy at the sentence level.

You are not writing self-help. You are writing the letter the reader's relationship has been waiting for somebody to send them.
